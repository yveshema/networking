IPv6 — NDP does ARP's job, and stops waking everyone up

Time: 45 min · Needs: three OL9 PCs on one Ethernet switch, Wireshark · GNS3 project: v6-a2-ndp

The question: IPv6 has no ARP. It also has no broadcast. Both statements are true, and yet a host still has to turn an address into a MAC before it can send anything. Find out what replaced it, and what the replacement bought.

Predict first

  1. H1 wants to reach H2 on the same wire. Something must resolve the address to a MAC. What? ___
  2. In IPv4, how many hosts on the segment have to process an ARP request? _
  3. In IPv6, how many should have to? _

Topology

     H1 ───────── SW ───────── H2
                   │
                   H3   (does nothing — it is the control)

Three OL9 PCs on one Ethernet switch, all with IPv4 addresses .11, .12, .13 and their automatic link-local IPv6 addresses from the previous activity. H3 is here only to be counted; it sends nothing all session.

Do this — the IPv4 baseline, one more time

Capture H3's link. On H1:

# on H1
ip neigh flush all
ping -c1 192.168.10.12

What just happened

  • H3 saw the ARP request, even though the conversation had nothing to do with it. You measured this cost in W3·A3: a broadcast is delivered to every host in the segment, and every one of them spends a little CPU deciding it is not the intended recipient.
  • The destination MAC was ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff. There is no way to be more specific, because at the moment ARP is sent the sender does not yet know any MAC to aim at. That is the trap ARP is stuck in, and it is why the request has to go to everyone.

Do this — the same job in IPv6

Keep the capture on H3 running. On H1:

# on H1
ip -6 neigh flush all
ping -c1 fe80::<H2's interface ID>%eth0

Now stop the capture and compare the two halves.

What just happened

  • H3 did not see it. Its capture has the ARP exchange from before and nothing from the IPv6 ping. The two hosts resolved each other's addresses without H3 being involved at all.
  • In the capture on the H1–H2 link you will find two ICMPv6 messages doing exactly ARP's job:
ICMPv6 type Asks / answers
Neighbour Solicitation 135 who has this address?
Neighbour Advertisement 136 I do, and here is my MAC
  • This is NDP — Neighbour Discovery Protocol. So "IPv6 has no ARP" is true in the narrow sense that the ARP protocol is gone, and misleading in every other sense: the job still exists and is still done, just by ICMPv6 messages instead of by a protocol of its own.

Do this — read the destination address

In the capture, find the Neighbour Solicitation and read its destination, at both layers.

What just happened

  • The IPv6 destination is not H2's address — H1 does not know it is reachable yet. It is something like ff02::1:ff00:12, a solicited-node multicast address, built by taking the last 24 bits of the address being looked for and pasting them onto a fixed prefix.
  • The Ethernet destination is not broadcast either. It is 33:33:ff:00:00:12 — a multicast MAC derived from the multicast IPv6 address in the same mechanical way.
  • Here is why that matters, and it is the whole point of the activity: a NIC that has not joined that multicast group discards the frame in hardware. H3's operating system was never interrupted, never scheduled, never asked to look at anything. The frame did not reach software.
  • Any host whose address happens to end in the same 24 bits will join the same group and will see the solicitation — so it is not perfectly targeted. But that is a handful of machines out of a segment of forty, instead of all forty.
  • IPv6 has no broadcast address at all. It was removed on purpose and multicast does its work, because "interrupt every host on the segment" turned out to be an expensive default. W3·A3 is the activity where you measured exactly how expensive.

Do this — the neighbour table has states

# on H1
ip -6 neigh show
ping -c1 fe80::<H2's interface ID>%eth0
ip -6 neigh show          # again, immediately

Wait a minute with no traffic, then run ip -6 neigh show once more.

What just happened

  • The entry moved between states: REACHABLE just after you used it, then STALE, and it will pass through DELAY and PROBE if it is used again after going stale.
  • Compare ip neigh show for IPv4, where entries are mostly REACHABLE or STALE and the machinery is far thinner. NDP actively tracks whether a neighbour is still there, rather than caching an answer and hoping.
  • That is called neighbour unreachability detection, and it is a second job ARP never did. When a machine is unplugged, an IPv6 host notices; an IPv4 host keeps sending into the void until its ARP entry ages out.
  • So NDP did not merely replace ARP. It absorbed ARP's job, dropped broadcast, and added a liveness check — three changes in one protocol.
