W3·A7 — CSMA, unplugged (no computers)

Time: 40 min · Needs: a room, one six-sided die per group, sticky notes · GNS3 project: none

The question: everything else this week was measured. This one cannot be — GNS3 models no shared medium, so no collision ever happens in it, and its hub does not collide either. So we run the protocol with people.

This is not a consolation prize. CSMA is a set of rules about timing and hearing, and a room full of people is a better instrument for that than any simulator.

Setup

Six to eight students per group, seated in a circle. The medium is speech in the room: only one person can be understood at a time, and everyone can hear everyone.

Each student gets three sticky notes — three frames to transmit. Each note has a destination written on it (another student's name). The round ends when every note has been delivered.

One student is the recorder and does not transmit. They track: attempts, collisions, successful deliveries, and the round's total elapsed time.

Round 1 — No protocol (ALOHA)

Rule: when you have a frame, say "[name], frame one" out loud. Whenever you like.

A transmission succeeds only if nobody else was speaking at any point during it. If two people overlap at all, both frames are lost and must be re-sent.

Recorder: count collisions.

Discuss

  • What is the collision count? What fraction of attempts succeeded?
  • What happened as the room got busier — did it get gradually worse, or did it fall off a cliff?
  • Nobody was doing anything wrong. The failures come from the rules, not the participants.

Round 2 — CSMA/CD (Ethernet on a shared medium)

Rules:

  1. Carrier Sense — before speaking, listen. If someone is talking, wait until they stop.
  2. Multiple Access — anyone may speak once it is quiet.
  3. Collision Detection — you can hear yourself and the room while speaking. If you hear another voice start during yours, stop immediately, mid-word. Do not finish.
  4. Backoff — after a collision, roll the die. Wait that many seconds before trying again. After a second collision on the same frame, roll and double the wait. Then double again. (This is binary exponential backoff, and the doubling is the whole trick.)

Same three frames each. Recorder: collisions, deliveries, total time.

Discuss

  • Collisions dropped sharply. Why did carrier sense not eliminate them entirely?
  • Answer: two people can both hear silence at the same moment and both start. Sensing removes collisions during a transmission, not collisions at the start of one. That residual window is irreducible, and it is exactly why detection and backoff are still needed.
  • Why stop mid-word? Finishing a frame you know is ruined wastes the medium for everyone. On real Ethernet, a station detecting a collision stops and sends a jam signal — deliberately making the damage unambiguous so every other station also gives up quickly.
  • Why double the wait? Repeated collisions mean the room is busy; backing off further is how the stations collectively find a rate the medium can carry, with no coordinator and no one able to count the others.
  • Where the "CD" went. Modern Ethernet is switched and full-duplex: every station has its own cable, and there is no shared medium to collide on. CSMA/CD is disabled on every link you have used this term. You have been learning the rules for a world that mostly ended around 2000 — which is worth knowing precisely, because the exam and the textbook both still assume it.

Round 3 — CSMA/CA and the hidden node (Wi-Fi)

Rearrange: one student is the access point in the centre. Two students sit at opposite ends of the room, facing away from each other, and are told to behave as though they genuinely cannot hear one another. Everyone can hear the AP.

Round 3a. Play by CSMA/CD's rules. The two distant students both have frames for the AP.

  • Both sense silence — correctly, from where they sit — and both transmit.
  • The AP hears a collision. Neither sender does. Both believe they succeeded.
  • This is the hidden node problem, and it is why Wi-Fi cannot use CSMA/CD. A radio cannot listen while transmitting on the same channel, and "the medium is quiet here" does not mean "the medium is quiet at the receiver" — which is the only place that matters.

Round 3b. Now add the rules Wi-Fi actually uses:

  1. Before transmitting, wait for silence plus a random number of slots (roll the die). Always, even when nothing is in the way.
  2. Send RTS — "AP, may I send?"
  3. The AP replies CTS — "go ahead" — and everyone in range hears the CTS, including the hidden node, who now stays quiet.
  4. Transmit.
  5. The AP sends ACK. No ACK means it did not arrive — that is the only way the sender ever finds out.

Discuss

  • Count the messages needed to deliver one frame: RTS, CTS, data, ACK. Four for one. That overhead is why advertised Wi-Fi speeds and real Wi-Fi speeds differ so much, and it is not waste — it is the price of not being able to hear a collision.
  • Why does CSMA/CA stand for Collision Avoidance? Because avoidance is all that is available: detection is impossible, so the protocol spends effort making collisions unlikely instead of cheap.
  • Round 2 stations only waited when the medium was busy. Round 3 stations wait every time. Why is that necessary here and not there?

Then, in your own words:

  1. Carrier sense reduced collisions but did not eliminate them. Why not?
  2. Why can Wi-Fi not detect collisions?
  3. Which of these three protocols runs on the switched links you built this week? (Careful.)

Instructor note

Question 3's answer is none of them — switched full-duplex Ethernet has no contention at all. Students who answer "CSMA/CD" have learned the textbook rather than the week. Let them say it, then ask which cable is shared.
