Week 3 — Ethernet and switching

Assumes: weeks 1–2. Students can read a capture, know a switch forwards on MAC addresses, and have already watched a redundant link destroy a network (W2·A5 Build 3).

Leaves students able to: print, flush and age a switch's forwarding table; distinguish broadcast flooding from unknown-unicast flooding; measure a broadcast domain; cut one in half with VLANs; and explain what spanning tree is for from having needed it.

The arc

Week 2 measured the switch from outside, because GNS3's Ethernet switch shows you nothing at all. This week replaces it with a Linux bridge on an OL9 Switch, where every piece of state is a command. The week is therefore structured as: here is the table you have been inferring since week 2 → here is what it does when it misses → here is its boundary → here is how to move the boundary → here is the protocol that keeps it from melting.

# Activity Time Needs
A1 The MAC address table ★ 45 min 1 OL9 Switch + 4 VPCS
A2 Flooding what it does not know 35 min A1 + 1 OL9 PC
A3 The broadcast domain, measured 25 min 4 OL9 PCs + 1 switch
A4 VLANs cut the segment ★ 45 min A1's topology
A5 Storm, then spanning tree ★ 50 min 2 OL9 Switches + 4 VPCS
A6 Filling the table (extension) 30 min Open vSwitch + Docker
A7 CSMA, unplugged (no computers) 40 min a room
A8 Configuration that survives ★ 80 min 2 OL9 Switches + 2 OL9 PCs + 1 VPCS

★ = keep if compressed. A6 and A7 are extensions and neither is assumed later.

Why a Linux bridge and not the built-in switch

The GNS3 Ethernet switch has no console, no CLI, and nothing in Configure beyond per-port VLANs. It behaves correctly and shows you nothing, which is fine for scenery and useless as a subject. A Linux bridge is the same software that switches packets on every KVM host in the world, runs on the same OS as the rest of the course, and prints every piece of its state.

Keep using the built-in switch wherever a switch is merely present — it costs almost no RAM.

The one thing GNS3 cannot do

CSMA/CD and CSMA/CA cannot be demonstrated here. GNS3 models no shared medium, so no collision ever occurs, and its hub does not collide either. A7 is an unplugged room activity, and it is better than a simulation would be. Packet Tracer can animate a collision, if you want the picture as well.

Deliverable

worksheet.md. Plus: the running project starts this week — see ../../project/milestone1-switching/. Milestone 1 is graded; this week's activities are not.

The relationship is deliberate: A1–A5 hand students every technique milestone 1 needs, on a topology of four hosts and one switch. Milestone 1 gives them requirements, three switches, no commands, and a network that has to survive a cable being pulled.
