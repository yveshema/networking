Week 4 — IPv4, the network layer and static routing

Assumes: weeks 1–3. Students can turn an OL9 node into a router (W2·A2), know that a router rewrites layer-2 addressing while leaving layer-3 alone, and have a VLAN'd network from week 3 that is deliberately unable to route between its segments.

Leaves students able to: read and predict a routing table; explain longest-prefix match by interrogating the kernel; diagnose a one-directional routing failure from two captures; account for every address change along a multi-hop path; connect two VLANs with a router; name the device that generated an ICMP message and say what decision it was reporting; and explain why a documented IPv4 feature can work perfectly in a lab and nowhere else.

The arc

Week 2 built a router whose subnets were all directly connected — it needed no routes, because every destination was out one of its own ports. This week is about the other case: destinations you are not attached to, which is every destination that matters.

The week's spine is a failure. A2 builds a network that is correctly configured at every visible point and works in one direction only, and the diagnosis requires captures at two places at once. Everything before it is preparation and everything after it is consequence.

The base topology is the course's standard one:

  LAN1 192.168.100.0/24      transit 10.0.0.0/30      LAN2 192.168.200.0/24

  PC1 ─── SW1 ─── R1 ═══════════════════════════ R2 ─── SW2 ─── PC2
  .10             .1  10.0.0.1         10.0.0.2  .1             .10
# Activity Time Needs

A2 The one-way route ★ 50 min 2 routers, 2 PCs, 2 switches
A3 What changes at each hop ★ 45 min A2's topology
A4 TTL, and what traceroute really is 35 min A2's topology
A5 Routing between VLANs 45 min 1 router, 1 switch, 4 VPCS
A6 The error channel ★ 45 min A2's topology
A7 Echo, and what ping does not prove 45 min A6 finished
A8 The options nobody uses ☆ 45 min A4 finished + a NAT node
A9 Forge the packet ☆ 50 min A6 + A7 finished; 3 PCs, 1 switch

★ = keep if compressed. ☆ = extension; homework or drop.

Plus one non-activity document: reading-why-protocol-extensions-die.md, assigned after A8. No commands, nothing to submit.

Sequencing note

A2 before A3. A2 produces a failure that A3 then explains completely. Reversed, A3 is a tour of header fields with no reason to care.

A5 closes week 3's open question — W3·A4 ended by proving a switch cannot move traffic between VLANs and postponing the fix. Students have been carrying that for a week. Pay it off.

A6 before A7, and both after A4. A4 teaches one ICMP message type and turns it into a tool without ever saying what ICMP is. A6 supplies the model — where ICMP sits inside IP, who generates a message, and the quoted-packet mechanism the whole protocol rests on. A7 then depends on that mechanism directly: path MTU discovery is unexplainable until students have seen an error quote their own packet back at them.

A8 after A4, and it needs the reading. A8's payoff is a comparison between A4's traceroute and the designed feature that was supposed to do the same job and lost. The activity states the conclusion; the reading is where it becomes a general rule about how the internet evolves. Assigning one without the other wastes both.

A9 caps A6 and A7, and it forges rather than reads. A6 and A7 build ICMP on paper — where it sits, what it quotes, how PMTU discovery uses that quote. A9 hands students scapy and has them construct the messages by hand, culminating in a forged fragmentation-needed error that changes a third host's path MTU without being either endpoint. It is the security payoff of A6's quoted-datagram lesson, and it needs both A6 and A7 done first or it lands as syntax with no meaning.

The second half of the week is about the network describing itself

A1–A5 are about forwarding: where a packet goes and why. A6–A9 are about the network's own commentary on that — the error channel, the query channel, a designed self-description mechanism that died, and finally forging the commentary itself to see that none of it is authenticated. Students who have only done A1–A5 can build a network. Students who have done A6–A9 can find out what is wrong with one, and why they cannot fully trust what it tells them.

RAM

A2 and A3 need four OL9 nodes — two Routers at 1 GB and two PCs at 512 MB, so ~3 GB, plus two Ethernet switches that cost nothing. Picking the PC template rather than a general-purpose node for PC1 and PC2 is where the saving comes from. This is still the peak of the term so far, and students on 8 GB machines running the GNS3 VM will be tight — see instructor.md.

A6, A7 and A8 all continue in w04-base and add no new nodes, so they cost nothing beyond what A2 already established. A8 additionally attaches a NAT node, which is free. A9 uses its own small three-node project (w04-a9-forge), not w04-base — three OL9 PCs on one switch, well under the week's peak.
