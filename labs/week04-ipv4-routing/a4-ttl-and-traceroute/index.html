W4·A4 — TTL, and what traceroute really is

Time: 35 min · Needs: A2's topology, working · GNS3 project: w04-base (continue)

The question: A3 showed the TTL counting down. What happens when it reaches zero, and why does that turn out to be the most useful diagnostic tool in networking?

Predict first

  1. PC1 sends a packet with TTL = 1 towards PC2. Where does it stop? _
  2. Does PC1 find out? ☐ yes ☐ no · If so, from whom? _
  3. TTL = 2? _

Do this — walk the path by hand

Start a capture on the PC1↔R1 link. Then:

# on PC1
ping -c1 -t 1 192.168.200.10
ping -c1 -t 2 192.168.200.10
ping -c1 -t 3 192.168.200.10
TTL Who answered What kind of message
1
2
3

What just happened

  • TTL 1: R1 answered — Time to live exceeded. R1 decremented 1 to 0, discarded the packet, and sent you a message saying so.
  • TTL 2: R2 answered, the same way.
  • TTL 3: PC2 answered normally, with an echo reply.
  • You just learned the identity of every device on the path, in order, without any of them being configured to tell you anything. Each one identified itself by the source address of its complaint.
  • The reply came from the router's own address — the interface the packet arrived on. That is why a traceroute shows you the near side of each router, and why the same router can appear under different addresses in traceroutes from different directions.

Do this — what it is actually for

TTL exists to stop loops, not to enable traceroute. Rebuild A2 part 4's routing loop:

# on R2
ip route del 192.168.100.0/24
ip route add 192.168.100.0/24 via 10.0.0.2

Capture the transit link, then from PC1: ping -c1 192.168.200.10.

What just happened

  • The packet bounced within R2 until the TTL hit zero, then died. The loop was self-limiting.
  • Compare with W3·A5's broadcast storm, where a loop at layer 2 multiplied without bound and took the network down. Same topology mistake, radically different outcome, because layer 3 has a hop counter and layer 2 has none.
  • That contrast is the best single argument for why the IP header carries a field that seems, at first sight, like pointless overhead on every packet ever sent.

Restore the correct route: ip route del 192.168.100.0/24; ip route add 192.168.100.0/24 via 10.0.0.1.

Do this — the automated version

# on PC1
tracepath 192.168.200.10

(traceroute if it is installed; tracepath is part of iputils and always present.)

What just happened

  • It is the loop you just ran by hand: TTL 1, TTL 2, TTL 3, collecting who complains at each step. Nothing more sophisticated is happening.
  • Knowing that tells you exactly when to distrust it:
  • A router configured not to send ICMP errors shows as *. That is not a broken hop — it is a silent one, and the path may be perfect.
  • Each probe is a separate packet that may take a different route. A traceroute is a sample of several journeys, not a photograph of one.
  • It shows the forward path only. After A2, you know that the return path can be completely different and completely broken, and traceroute will not tell you.
  • tracepath also reports the MTU along the way, which is week 1's -M do experiment automated in the same style.

Do this — one asymmetry worth seeing

# on PC1
tracepath 192.168.200.10

# on PC2
tracepath 192.168.100.10

What just happened

  • The two lists are not mirror images: each shows the interface addresses facing towards the sender. R1 appears as 192.168.100.1 from one side and 10.0.0.1 from the other, and both are the same router.
  • A single machine having several addresses, and answering by whichever one faces you, is normal and is why "the router's IP address" is not a well-formed phrase.
