Reading — Why protocol extensions die

Not an activity. Background for W4·A8, best read after it. No commands, nothing to submit. It takes one dead header field and turns it into the single most useful thing you can know about how the internet actually changes.

The room that was left on purpose

The people who designed the internet's addressing in the 1970s knew they could not foresee what it would need. So they left room. Every packet carries a header — a small block of bookkeeping at the front — and they made that header able to grow. A packet could carry extra instructions: record the address of every router you pass through, or stamp the time at each hop, or take this specific route rather than the one you would normally choose.

This was good design. It meant the network could gain new abilities later without replacing everything that already existed.

And it worked, for a while. Those extras were real, useful, and used.

Today they are effectively dead. Not removed from the standard — the standard still describes them, and the lab network you built in A8 handles them perfectly. They died in the real world, and how they died is worth more than the features themselves.

Three forces

Speed. Routers became fast by making the common case predictable. An ordinary packet header is always the same size and always laid out the same way, so the decision about where to send it can be made by dedicated hardware in a fraction of a microsecond. A packet carrying extras is a different shape — variable length, requiring interpretation. It cannot take that fast path. It is handed to a general-purpose processor instead, which is not slightly slower but thousands of times slower.

So a packet using the extension mechanism costs the network far more to carry than a normal one. Multiply that by the volume a backbone router handles and it stops being an inefficiency and becomes a way to overwhelm expensive equipment using very little effort.

Space. The room left for extras was capped at 40 bytes. Record Route spends four bytes per router plus a little bookkeeping — nine routers, maximum, which you calculated yourself in A8. When the design was written, nine was generous. On today's internet a path of fifteen or twenty hops is unremarkable. The feature could not describe the network it was meant to describe.

Trust. One of the extras let the sender dictate the route its packet would take. That sounds harmless. It is not. Nearly every protection a network has rests on an assumption about where traffic came from — this arrived on the outside link, so treat it as untrusted; that arrived on the inside link, so trust it more. If the sender controls the path, the sender controls which link the packet appears to arrive on, and every one of those assumptions can be deliberately arranged to be wrong. Once people noticed, operators switched it off everywhere.

The loop that finished them off

Now follow the incentives.

Each of those three forces gives a network operator a good reason to discard packets carrying extras. None of them is a conspiracy. Each is an entirely local, entirely sensible decision made by someone with a network to run.

But once enough operators make it, a packet using extras is no longer reliably deliverable. So software stops using them — because software that depends on something unreliable is software that fails.

And once nothing uses them, discarding them costs nothing. There is no longer any traffic to break. Which makes the decision easier still for the next operator.

That loop is the whole story. The capability was never withdrawn. It was written out of reality by a million independent, individually reasonable choices. The document still says it works. The network says otherwise, and the network wins.

The general law

This generalises far beyond one header:

Anything a protocol permits but rarely uses will eventually stop working.

Not by decision. By disuse. Equipment sitting between two computers — firewalls, load balancers, carrier gear, home routers — only reliably handles what it sees every day. Anything rare is anything untested, and anything untested is, sooner or later, broken. Rarity is not a symptom of the failure. It is the cause.

The name for this is ossification: the protocol hardens into the shape of whatever traffic is common, and the written specification drifts away from the working one. The internet is not what its standards say. It is the intersection of what its standards say and what the equipment in the middle happens to tolerate.

It happened again

When IPv6 was designed, all of this was already understood. The response was structural: make the main header a fixed size — always the same, always fast — and move every extension into a separate chain behind it, so the fast path stays fast no matter what a packet is carrying.

Extensions of that kind are now widely discarded in transit. Measurement studies keep finding that a meaningful share of the internet simply drops packets that carry them.

The redesign did not help, and the reason is the important part: the format was never the problem. The problem was the incentive. Equipment in the middle gains nothing from handling rare traffic and loses nothing by discarding it. Change the format and that incentive is untouched.

What actually worked

Given all that, only two strategies have succeeded, and both rest on the same insight: stop asking the middle of the network to cooperate.

Hide the change. If equipment in the middle cannot see a field, it cannot object to it. So encrypt everything you intend to keep evolving. The newest transport protocols encrypt not only your data but almost all of their own bookkeeping. Privacy is part of the motivation; the larger part is preserving the ability to change things later without needing permission from hardware nobody controls.

Wear a familiar shape. Build the new thing so it looks exactly like something the network already carries in enormous volume and passes without inspection. QUIC — the transport protocol that now carries a large share of the web — is a genuinely new design that deliberately presents itself as ordinary, unremarkable traffic of a kind every network already forwards without a second look. It did not win an argument about what networks ought to support. It arranged for the argument never to happen.

Back to the two traceroutes

You built the same tool twice. Once as the designed feature — ask the network to record its own path — and once as the workaround from A4: send packets rigged to expire one hop earlier each time, and collect the complaints.

The designed feature is elegant, purpose-built, and dead. The workaround is a hack, and it is what every network engineer on earth uses.

The workaround won because it asks the network for nothing new. No cooperation, no capability, no permission. It needs routers to do the one thing they already do when a packet runs out of hops: complain about it. So it works through equipment that has never heard of traceroute and would refuse it if asked.

That is the lesson, in one line:

Mechanisms that require the network to cooperate die. Mechanisms that exploit what the network already does survive.

It is why the internet is so hard to improve, and why the improvements that do succeed almost always arrive disguised as something that was already there.
