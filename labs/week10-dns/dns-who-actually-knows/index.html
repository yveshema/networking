DNS — Who actually knows the answer

Time: 50 min · Needs: one OL9 PC, one Ethernet switch, a NAT node for egress · GNS3 project: dn-a2-delegation

The question: the previous activity asked one server and got one answer, in one round trip. But nobody holds a list of every name on the internet — such a list could not exist, could not be distributed, and nobody would agree on who ran it. So where did your answer come from? Walk the path yourself, one server at a time.

This activity needs real internet access. Attach a NAT node to the switch, as in W4·A8, and confirm ping 1.1.1.1 works before starting.

Predict first

  1. Does the server in your /etc/resolv.conf know the address of example.com? ☐ yes ☐ no
  2. If not, how many other servers were involved before you got an answer? _
  3. Who is allowed to decide what example.com resolves to? _

Do this — let something else do the walking, once

# on CLIENT
dig +trace example.com A

Read it top to bottom. It is long, and it is a transcript of a search.

What just happened

  • Several stages, each one narrowing. Roughly: the root, then the servers for com., then the servers for example.com., and only then an address.
  • This is the shape of the whole system. DNS is not a database, it is a hierarchy of responsibility, and each level knows only two things: the small part it is responsible for, and who to ask about everything below it.

Now do the same walk yourself, so you see what each server actually says.

Do this — ask the root

# on CLIENT
dig @a.root-servers.net example.com A

What just happened

  • There is no ANSWER section. The root server did not answer your question, and it is not being unhelpful — it genuinely does not know and never will.
  • What came back instead is a referral: an AUTHORITY section naming the servers for com., and an ADDITIONAL section giving their addresses.
  • Those addresses in ADDITIONAL have a name — glue records — and they solve a circularity. The servers for com. are themselves called things like a.gtld-servers.net, and finding that address requires DNS, which requires finding a server, which requires DNS. The referral therefore ships the addresses along with the names, so the search can continue without starting a second one.
  • There are thirteen root server names and a great many actual machines behind them. They are the one part of DNS whose addresses every resolver must know in advance, because there is nothing above them to ask. Every recursive resolver on earth ships with that list baked in.

Do this — follow the referral, twice

Take a server name from the AUTHORITY section and ask it:

# on CLIENT
dig @a.gtld-servers.net example.com A

Then take a server from that answer's AUTHORITY section and ask it:

# on CLIENT
dig @a.iana-servers.net example.com A

What just happened

  • The com. servers also declined to answer, and referred you again — this time to the servers responsible for example.com itself.
  • The last one answered. Look at the flags line and find aa: authoritative answer. It is present only here, and only from these servers, because these are the machines the owner of the domain designated. Everyone else in the chain was a signpost.
  • Three questions to three different organisations produced one address, and no single one of them could have. The answer is not stored anywhere central; it is stored by whoever owns the name, and the hierarchy exists to route your question to them.

Do this — see the delegation from above and below

# on CLIENT
dig @a.gtld-servers.net example.com NS     # what the parent says
dig @a.iana-servers.net example.com NS     # what the zone itself says

What just happened

  • The parent zone (com.) holds NS records saying this name is delegated to those servers. The child zone holds its own NS records saying the same thing.
  • Two copies of the same fact, maintained by two different parties. The parent's copy is what everybody actually follows, because it is what a resolver walking down encounters first. If the two disagree — because the owner moved servers but the registrar was never told — you get a domain that resolves for some people and not others, and it is a genuinely nasty fault to diagnose.
  • Notice what "owning a domain" actually means, mechanically: it means the parent zone contains NS records pointing at servers you control. That is the whole of it. Registration is the administrative process for getting those two lines into the parent.

Do this — recursive versus iterative

Compare what you just did with what your own resolver does:

# on CLIENT
dig example.com A                    # your configured resolver
dig +norecurse example.com A         # ask it not to do the work

What just happened

  • The normal query set a recursion desired flag. Your resolver did the entire walk you just did by hand — possibly a dozen packets — and returned one answer. That mode is called recursive, and it is the service a resolver provides.
  • The walk you did yourself, one referral at a time, is iterative. Every server in the chain answers iteratively; only your resolver works recursively on your behalf.
  • With +norecurse you likely got either nothing useful or a cached copy. A server that will do recursion for anyone who asks is an open resolver, and it is a liability — it can be used to amplify attacks against third parties, since a small query can produce a large answer sent to a forged address.

If it went wrong

Symptom Cause Fix
Everything times out no internet egress attach and start the NAT node; confirm ping 1.1.1.1 first
dig @a.root-servers.net fails to resolve the server's own name your resolver is broken too use an address: dig @198.41.0.4 example.com A
+trace produces one line and stops no route, or UDP 53 blocked upstream check egress; some networks intercept port 53 entirely
Different server names than shown here the set changes, and example.com is a real domain use whatever names the referral gives you — the structure is the point, not the names
No aa flag anywhere you never reached the authoritative servers follow the referrals to the end
Answers come back instantly and identically your resolver cached the whole chain that is the next activity
